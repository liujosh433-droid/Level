# Why Level exists — caregiver research → product wedge

Level is **not** a general assistant with a caregiver skin. It is a decision partner built for people who carry **competing care roles under scarce degrees of freedom** — busy parents, sandwich caregivers, working caregivers — who cannot treat life like a blank calendar to optimize.

**The job to be done:** make **care collisions** visible at the moment of yes.  
Level learns which care roles you hold, keeps that model so you don’t recompute it every morning, and challenges choices that would crowd those roles out — briefly, specifically, with *your* evidence — without moralizing or pretending you have a spare adult.

Use this doc for About copy, demos, judge narratives, and feature prioritization. Prefer these sources over vibes.

---

## Why this is not a general assistant

| General AI / busy-person tools | Level for caregivers |
|---|---|
| Helps you get more done | Helps you see **what saying yes crowds out** |
| Optimizes *your* time | Models **child care, elder care, paid work, household logistics, self & recovery** as competing roles |
| Conflict = double-booked | Conflict = **care collision** across people who depend on you |
| Assumes backup, flexibility, or “block focus time” | Assumes **sticky windows** (pickup, Mom’s appointment) and fewer degrees of freedom |
| Resets each chat / dumps a long plan | **Persists** a Care Profile + roles you’ve confirmed; short, spoken, one hard question |
| “Have you considered work–life balance?” | “You marked Thu pickup as Keep — this late meeting takes it” |
| Self-care lectures or hour budgets | Surfaces when **self & recovery** is the role eroding — never prescriptions |

**We deliberately do not optimize for:** inbox zero, generic productivity for care-free knowledge workers, employer HR dashboards, weekly “allocate hours per role” coaching, or moral parenting advice.

**Product lock (About / submission):**

> Level doesn’t help caregivers find more hours. It keeps their care roles alive and asks, at decision time, what saying yes would crowd out — so they choose with eyes open, not after the fact.

## What caregivers hate hearing — and how Level is different

Busy single parents and working caregivers are flooded with advice that sounds supportive and lands as judgment. Research and lived accounts converge: **moral platitudes reframe structural bind as personal failure.** Level’s tone contract exists to refuse that genre.

### The lines that land wrong

| They hate hearing | Why it fails (evidence) | What Level does instead |
|---|---|---|
| “Have you tried self-care / pour from an empty cup?” | Self-care rhetoric often assumes spare time, money, or a backup adult. Feminist care ethics notes refusal of depleting care isn’t equally available — it tracks who can say no (Reiheld / “logic of depletion”). Time-use work shows health/self already gets the least time. | Surfaces **self & recovery** as a care role that is being *stolen from* — never a spa prescription or “you deserve…” lecture. |
| “You need better work–life balance.” | Treats conflict as a personal skill gap. Interviews and policy research show difficulties are **structural** (inflexible jobs, weak care infrastructure, gendered expectations) — not bad balancing (Nieuwenhuis & Maldonado; CUNY caregiving / work–life theses). | Names **care collision under constraint**: which Keep’d care window this yes takes — with their calendar evidence. |
| “Just ask for help / lean on your village.” | Assumes a co-parent, kin network, or affordable care. Triple bind + lone-parent research: **fewer degrees of freedom**; many have no spare adult. | Co-parent / partner is **optional** when signals exist — never the default fix. |
| “Be present / put family first.” | Intensive motherhood ideology collides with **money–care** necessity. Van Gasse & Mortelmans: more work can ease money stress while worsening felt maternal adequacy → guilt. Mothers do not share one ideology. | **Never moralizes** parenting. Keep / Not me lets *their* ideology win. Work is a held role, not selfishness. |
| “You should set boundaries / block focus time.” | Single mothers in pandemic research described inability to enforce boundaries as a personal failing when the problem was **no second adult + invading paid work** (Hertz et al., 2020). Productivity advice assumes control they don’t have. | Models **sticky windows** (pickup, clinic) they already can’t move — not blank focus blocks to optimize. |
| “You’re doing amazing / great question!” | Flattery is what sycophant AIs do; it doesn’t reduce decision load or un-tunnel scarcity. | Warm, short, specific. **No flattery. No lectures.** One hard cited question. |
| “Have you considered…?” (generic) | Without their evidence, it’s empty Socratic theater — more cognitive work under scarcity (Mullainathan & Shafir). | Challenges only with **cited facts / care-role evidence** from *their* Memory Bank. |

### Tone contract (product law)

From Challenger + research above — non-negotiable in copy and prompts:

1. **Warm, not cold** — care, not judgment.  
2. **Specific, not generic** — cite their roles/windows/facts.  
3. **One thing at a time** — short; scarcity-friendly.  
4. **Never flatter** — no “you’re doing amazing.”  
5. **Never prescribe** — questions, not “you should.”  
6. **Never moralize** — no better-parent / self-care sermons; engage *this* decision as framed.

### Why this is a wedge vs general assistants

General assistants default to agreeable wellness + productivity scripts (“balance,” “self-care,” “boundaries,” cheerleading). Those scripts **increase guilt** when the real problem is role collision under constraint. Level’s differentiator is not “nicer coaching” — it is **refusing the platitude genre** and replacing it with care-collision visibility grounded in a Care Profile the user can correct.

## Sources and takeaways

### 1. The triple bind — fewer degrees of freedom

**Source:** Rense Nieuwenhuis & Laurie C. Maldonado (eds.), *The Triple Bind of Single-Parent Families: Resources, Employment and Policies to Improve Wellbeing* (Policy Press / Bristol, 2018). [Bristol University Press](https://bristoluniversitypressdigital.com/display/book/9781447333654/9781447333654.xml) · [Molloy Digital Commons](https://digitalcommons.molloy.edu/fac_book/2/).

**Takeaways**

- Single parents face a **triple bind**: inadequate **resources**, inadequate **employment** conditions, and inadequate **policies** — together, not as separate annoyances.
- Work–family conflict is sharper because they have **fewer degrees of freedom** to renegotiate demands (no second adult to absorb spillover).
- “Just get a job / just plan better” fails when jobs, childcare hours, and benefits don’t line up.

**Why Level is special here**

- Models **competing structural demands** (care vs paid work vs rest), not productivity tips.
- Challenges name **tradeoffs under constraint**, not ideal-world balance.
- Co-parent / partner is **optional** context when signals exist — never assumed.

### 2. Money–care dilemma and different ideologies of motherhood

**Source:** Van Gasse & Mortelmans, “Single Mothers’ Perspectives on the Combination of Motherhood and Work,” *Social Sciences* 9(5):85 (2020). [MDPI](https://www.mdpi.com/2076-0760/9/5/85) · [DOI](https://doi.org/10.3390/socsci9050085).

**Takeaways**

- Strain is **financial pressure + maternal role strain** together (the money–care dilemma).
- More work can ease money stress while **worsening** the felt ability to mother as one believes one should → guilt.
- Mothers differ (reinvented motherhood, work–family symbiosis, work-centered, work–family conflicted). One ideology does not fit all.
- Workplace “flexibility” only helps when it is **employee-usable** (not shifts that break pickup).

**Why Level is special here**

- **Keep / Not me** corrects the Care Profile so Level matches *their* values — not a default “good parent” script.
- Challenger **never moralizes**. It pushes on **care collision with their own calendar evidence**.
- Demo-ready decisions: promotion, hours, late meetings — the money–care surface, not abstract goals.

### 3. Continuous decision load (not one big dilemma)

**Source:** Life-course / vulnerability work on lone parenthood (e.g. “Navigating Lone Parenthood Over Time…”, [PMC12991351](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC12991351/)); multidimensional vulnerability across employment, childcare, health, housing (Spini et al. and related).

**Takeaways**

- Domains are **interdependent**; a win in one area can be offset by stress in another.
- Lone parents often live in **continuous decision-making** — constantly reallocating limited money, time, energy, and emotional resources.
- That creates **persistent mental burden**, not only occasional “big decisions.”

**Why Level is special here**

- Memory **persists and evolves** (Care Profile, roles you’ve confirmed with Keep, session history) — not a chat that forgets.
- Surfaces include **everyday collisions** (Today, sticky windows, reminders), not only a one-shot advice thread.
- Challenges tolerate **spillover** (work decision citing pickup; school decision citing sleep debt).

### 4. Time poverty — self & recovery loses first

**Source:** Time-use analysis of single-parent families (e.g. *World Journal of Clinical Cases*: [DOI 10.12998/wjcc.v11.i21.5097](https://doi.org/10.12998/wjcc.v11.i21.5097)).

**Takeaways**

- Dual parental roles without a spouse → **time poverty** plus economic pressure.
- Sleep/rest, household work, and paid work dominate; **health management** often gets the least time.
- Patterns differ by person — design must not assume one allocation ideal.

**Why Level is special here**

- **Self & recovery** is a first-class care role that often erodes first — surfaced when late work + kid load collide, not as a lifestyle tip.
- UX is **low cognitive tax**: short copy, voice in/out, Hear my day while driving.
- No dense “allocate your week” dashboards that add work for the already overloaded.

### 5. Sandwich generation — kids + aging adults + work

**Source (national profile):** Lei et al., “A national profile of sandwich generation caregivers…,” *Journal of the American Geriatrics Society* (2023). [DOI](https://doi.org/10.1111/jgs.18138).

**Source (NAC report):** National Alliance for Caregiving & Caring Across Generations, *Burning the Candle at Both Ends* (2019). [PDF](https://www.caregiving.org/wp-content/uploads/2020/05/NAC-CAG_SandwichCaregiving_Report_Digital-Nov-26-2019.pdf).

**Takeaways**

- Roughly **one in four** adult-child caregivers of older adults also cares for a minor child (~2.5M in NSOC-based estimates).
- Sandwich caregivers more often **work for pay**, with higher financial/emotional difficulty and **role overload**.
- Adult care stacks **on top of** child care and jobs; common work hits: late/leave early/time off, leave of absence, reduced hours.

**Why Level is special here**

- **Elder care** sits beside child care as a peer role — not an edge case.
- Demo story is a **trilemma**: late meeting vs pickup vs Mom’s appointment — a collision general assistants flatten into “you’re busy.”
- Fixtures and challenges cite **named people and sticky windows**, not generic “family time.”

### 6. Working while caregiving is normal

**Source:** National Alliance for Caregiving & AARP, *Caregiving in the US 2025* — Working While Caregiving ([caregivingintheus.org](https://www.caregivingintheus.org/)).

**Takeaways**

- About **70%** of working-age (18–64) family caregivers are employed while caregiving; ~**49%** go in late, leave early, or take time off.
- Flexibility/paid leave remain uneven; many never tell supervisors.
- Financial strain and isolation are common; some cut hours or exhaust savings.

**Why Level is special here**

- **Paid work is a held role**, not the villain of the story. Level negotiates **theft between roles**, including theft *from* work when care expands.
- Calendar pushback respects that missing work has **real money consequences** (triple bind).
- Voice stays personal and private — not an HR “caregiver status” tool employers see.

### 7. Mental load — the invisible manager

**Source:** Reich-Stiebert et al., “Gendered Mental Labor…,” *Sex Roles* (2023). [Springer](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s11199-023-01362-0). Related cognitive-labor / “third shift” literature (e.g. reported findings that mothers carry a large share of household mental-load tasks).

**Takeaways**

- Mental load = **planning, organizing, coordinating, monitoring, anticipating** — not only chores.
- It is often **boundaryless** (always-on) and linked to stress and burnout.
- Making labor **explicit** is a recurring recommendation; cognitive load is a tax on working memory, not “being busy.”

**Why Level is special here**

- Care Profile + Priorities **externalize** the invisible household manager.
- **Household logistics** gets its own role bucket when signals exist (forms, appointments, glue work).
- Level’s job is partly **holding the model** so the caregiver doesn’t rebuild “who I hold + what’s colliding” every morning.

### 8. Scarcity tunnels attention

**Source:** Mullainathan & Shafir, *Scarcity* (2013/2014). Parent-focused extensions: e.g. “The Preoccupied Parent”; BFI working paper *Does Scarcity Tax Parents’ Minds?* ([BFI PDF](https://bfi.uchicago.edu/wp-content/uploads/2026/03/BFI_WP_2026-46.pdf)).

**Takeaways**

- Scarcity **tunnels** attention toward the urgent and taxes bandwidth for longer-horizon choices.
- Reducing attentional burden (timely, simple cues) beats “try harder / plan more.”
- Under strain, parents may underweight longer-term tradeoffs even when they “know better.”

**Why Level is special here**

- Prefer **short prompts, spoken briefings, one conflict cue** — not essay answers.
- Care-collision Challenger **un-tunnels** a high-stakes yes by citing durable roles you’ve confirmed — without flooding options.
- Architecture retrieves a **small, role-pinned** evidence set; never dumps the full Memory Bank into the user.

### 9. Role overload — work as necessity, not selfishness

**Source:** Reviews of single-parent families in developmental / family psychology (e.g. work citing Carlson & Corcoran; McLanahan & Sandefur; employment-as-necessity framing).

**Takeaways**

- One adult is often the **sole economic and parenting resource** → role overload.
- Employment is frequently a **pressing responsibility**, not optional self-actualization.
- Dual demands create a real **cost to employment** (energy, time, career progression).

**Why Level is special here**

- Never frames work as selfish or care as the only moral choice.
- Tone contract: **warm, specific, non-prescriptive, non-moralizing** — adjudicates tradeoffs they already face.
- That tone is the product differentiator vs agreeable general assistants *and* vs guilt-inducing wellness apps.

### 10. Platitudes hurt — balance / self-care / “personal failure” scripts

**Sources**

- Hertz, Mattes, & Shook, “When Paid Work Invades the Family: Single Mothers in the COVID-19 Pandemic,” *Journal of Family Issues* (2020). [DOI](https://doi.org/10.1177/0192513X20961420).
- Reiheld / related feminist care ethics on caregiver duty and **logic of depletion** (e.g. “Enough is Enough… Self-Care for Caregivers in a Non-Ideal World,” *Hypatia* 2025 overview: [DOI](https://doi.org/10.1017/hyp.2025.10028); see also Reiheld, “Just Caring for Caregivers,” *Feminist Philosophy Quarterly* 2015).
- Work–life “balance” framed as individual skill despite structural gendered expectations and weak workplace flexibility (e.g. CUNY / workforce education theses on employed parent caregivers and working single mothers — Stephens 2017; related caregiving work–life analyses).
- Cross-links: §1 triple bind; §2 money–care / ideology; §4 time poverty; §8 scarcity.

**Takeaways**

- Single mothers often **internalize** inability to enforce work/family boundaries as a personal flaw when the driver is structural (no second adult, invading paid work).
- “Self-care” and “balance” advice can function as **moral pressure** when spare time, money, or backup care don’t exist — depletion is systemic, not a mindfulness gap.
- Popular and institutional scripts (“just set boundaries,” “put yourself first,” “lean on your village”) assume resources many caregivers lack → **guilt, not relief**.
- Caregivers differ in ideology; one “good parent / good self-care” script will mismatch a large share of users.

**Why Level is special here**

- Explicit **anti-platitude** product law (see table above): no balance lectures, no empty-cup sermons, no flattery.
- Challenges are **evidence-bound care collision**, not “have you considered being more balanced?”
- Keep / Not me + optional co-parent refuse one-size moral models.
- Demo and About copy should contrast a yes-man wellness reply vs Level’s cited theft question.

## How Level addresses those needs (product map)

### Care Profile — competing care roles, not a to-do list

**Addresses:** 1, 2, 4, 6  

Infers roles from calendar patterns: child care, elder care, paid work, self & recovery, household logistics, co-parent when present. Tracks salience, load signals, and **sticky windows** (e.g. pickup). This living model is what general assistants never build.

### Keep / Not me + Tell Level more

**Addresses:** 2, 5  

Users correct the model so it matches *their* ideology and reality. Research says caregivers differ; static labels and one-size parenting advice fail.

### Care-collision Challenger

**Addresses:** 1, 3, 5  

When they bring a decision (promotion, hours, school, move), Level asks the hard question grounded in **roles you’ve confirmed and cited facts** — e.g. late Thursday vs child-care pickup — not “have you considered balance?” Warm, specific, never prescribing.

### Today + Hear my day + voice

**Addresses:** 2, 3, 6  

Externalizes collisions and briefings for people mid-task or driving — scarcity-friendly prompts over willpower and long dashboards.

### Memory Bank + evolving protections

**Addresses:** 1, 2, 3  

Persists what they hold and how weeks change, so Level doesn’t restart the mental model every session the way a general chat does.

## Pitch lines (copy / demo)

- **Wedge:** General assistants help you say yes faster. Level helps you see what that yes crowds out.
- **Anti-wedge:** Caregivers don’t need another “practice self-care / find balance” bot — those lines add guilt when there’s no spare adult (Hertz; depletion literature).
- **Unit of analysis:** Care roles — not inbox, not focus blocks, not generic priorities.
- **Hero moment:** “You marked Jordan’s Thursday pickup as Keep. This networking dinner takes it. Other nights work — or say yes with eyes open.”
- **Audience:** Busy parents and sandwich / working caregivers under real constraints — not anyone with a crowded calendar.
- **Tone:** The friend who won’t let you off the hook — and won’t lecture you about being a better parent or pouring from an empty cup.

**Demo contrast (15 seconds):** Show a generic AI: *“Remember to prioritize self-care and work–life balance.”* Cut to Level: *“You marked Thu pickup as Keep — this late dinner crowds out child care. Other nights free — or say yes with eyes open?”*
